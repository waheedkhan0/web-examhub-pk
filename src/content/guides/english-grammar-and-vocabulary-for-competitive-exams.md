## Why English Determines Competitive Merit in Pakistan

Across all federal and provincial competitive examinations in Pakistan—including the **CSS English Précis and Composition** paper, the **PMS English Compulsory**, and the 20-to-50 mark English sections in **FPSC**, **PPSC**, and **NTS** screenings—English is the primary determinant of success.

Examiners do not expect ornate or flowery Victorian prose. Instead, they rigorously evaluate **syntactic precision, grammatical correctness, structural parallelism, and nuanced vocabulary application**.

Below is our editorial board’s comprehensive guide to the **five most frequently tested grammatical principles** and the **etymological root-word system** for vocabulary acquisition.

---

### Part 1: The 5 High-Yield Grammatical Rules Tested by Examiners

#### Rule 1: Complex Subject-Verb Agreement Dilemmas
The most common grammatical pitfall in screening tests involves subjects separated from their verbs by prepositional phrases or relative clauses:

* **Intervening Prepositional Phrases:** The verb must agree strictly with the primary head noun, ignoring intervening phrases (*along with, as well as, in addition to, together with*).
  * *Incorrect:* *"The Prime Minister, along with his entire federal cabinet, **have** arrived."*
  * *Correct:* *"The Prime Minister, along with his entire federal cabinet, **has** arrived."* (The singular subject is *The Prime Minister*).
* **Indefinite Pronouns Rule:** Pronouns such as *each, everyone, someone, anybody, neither, either* are grammatically singular in formal English:
  * *Incorrect:* *"Each of the candidates **have** completed their examination."*
  * *Correct:* *"Each of the candidates **has** completed his or her examination."*
* **The Proximity Rule with Correlative Conjunctions:** When subjects are joined by *either... or*, *neither... nor*, or *not only... but also*, the verb must agree with the subject **closest to it**:
  * *Correct:* *"Neither the director nor the **officers were** present."*
  * *Correct:* *"Neither the officers nor the **director was** present."*

#### Rule 2: Conditional Sentences & The Subjunctive Mood
Examiners in CSS and PPSC frequently test the four conditional structures and hypothetical expressions:

| Conditional Type | Condition Clause ("If" Clause) | Main Result Clause | Practical Exam Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Zero Conditional** (Scientific truth) | Simple Present | Simple Present | *"If water reaches 100°C, it boils."* |
| **First Conditional** (Real future possibility) | Simple Present | Will / Can / May + Bare Infinitive | *"If the government enforces fiscal discipline, tax revenues will increase."* |
| **Second Conditional** (Hypothetical present/future) | Simple Past (were) | Would / Could / Might + Bare Infinitive | *"If I **were** the governor (not was), I **would** lower the benchmark policy rate."* |
| **Third Conditional** (Unfulfilled past condition) | Past Perfect (had + V3) | Would have / Could have + Past Participle | *"If the irrigation department **had repaired** the barrage, the agricultural lands **would not have flooded**."* |

* **The Subjunctive with Verbs of Urgency:** Verbs such as *demand, insist, recommend, propose, suggest* take the subjunctive base form without an "-s" or modal auxiliary:
  * *Incorrect:* *"The committee recommended that the commissioner **resigns**."*
  * *Correct:* *"The committee recommended that the commissioner **resign**."*

---

#### Rule 3: Eliminating Dangling & Misplaced Modifiers
A modifier must be placed immediately adjacent to the specific noun or pronoun it logically describes. A **dangling modifier** occurs when the intended subject is absent from the sentence:

* *Incorrect (Dangling):* *"Walking into the civil secretariat, the historic clock tower caught my eye."* 
  * *(This sentence grammatically implies that the clock tower was walking into the secretariat).*
* *Correct:* *"Walking into the civil secretariat, **I noticed** the historic clock tower."* or *"As I walked into the civil secretariat, the historic clock tower caught my eye."*

---

#### Rule 4: Structural Parallelism in Compound Constructions
When ideas are listed in a series or connected by coordinating conjunctions (*and, but, or*) or correlative pairs (*not only... but also*), all elements must share the identical grammatical form:

* *Incorrect:* *"The commissioner was praised for his administrative integrity, working diligently, and because he was honest."*
  * *(Mixes noun phrase, participle phrase, and subordinate clause).*
* *Correct (Parallel Nouns):* *"The commissioner was praised for his **administrative integrity**, **diligent work**, and **uncompromising honesty**."*

---

#### Rule 5: Correct Usage of Confusing Fixed Prepositions
Fixed preposition errors appear in almost every PPSC and FPSC screening paper:

| Verb / Adjective | Correct Preposition | Incorrect Variant to Avoid |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Abstain / Refrain** | **from** (*"abstain from voting"*) | *abstain to vote* |
| **Averse** | **to** (*"averse to risk"*) | *averse from risk* |
| **Comply** | **with** (*"comply with regulations"*) | *comply to regulations* |
| **Congratulate** | **on** (*"congratulated him on his success"*) | *congratulated him for* |
| **Deprive** | **of** (*"deprived of rights"*) | *deprived from* |
| **Prefer** | **to** (*"prefer tea to coffee"*) | *prefer tea than coffee* |
| **Accountable** | **to** an authority / **for** an action | *accountable with* |

### Part 2: Building a 1,000-Word Vocabulary Through Etymology

Attempting to memorize thousands of dictionary words alphabetically is ineffective. The scientific method for vocabulary acquisition is **Etymology**—learning Latin and Greek roots, prefixes, and suffixes. A single root unlocks the meanings of dozens of related academic words.

#### 5 High-Yield Latin & Greek Roots for Competitive Exams

1. **BENE / BON (Good, Well):**
   * *Benevolent:* Well-meaning and kindly.
   * *Benefactor:* A person who gives financial or other support to a cause.
   * *Beneficent:* Generous or doing good.
   * *Benign:* Gentle, non-harmful (opposed to *Malignant*).
2. **MAL (Bad, Evil):**
   * *Malevolent:* Having or showing a wish to do evil to others.
   * *Malfeasance:* Wrongdoing, especially by a public official.
   * *Malaise:* A general feeling of discomfort, illness, or unease.
   * *Maladroit:* Inept, clumsy, or unskillful.
3. **LOQ / LOC (Speak, Talk):**
   * *Eloquent:* Fluent or persuasive in speaking or writing.
   * *Loquacious:* Tending to talk a great deal; talkative.
   * *Circumlocution:* The use of many words where fewer would do, especially in a deliberate attempt to be vague.
   * *Colloquial:* Used in ordinary or familiar conversation; not formal or literary.
4. **CHRON (Time):**
   * *Anachronistic:* Belonging or appropriate to a period other than that in which it exists.
   * *Chronic:* Persisting for a long time or constantly recurring.
   * *Synchronize:* Occur or operate at the same time or rate.
5. **PLAC / PLEAS (To Please, Soothe):**
   * *Placate:* Make someone less angry or hostile; appease.
   * *Implacable:* Unable to be placated or appeased; relentless.
   * *Placid:* Calm, peaceful, and not easily upset.
   * *Complacent:* Showing smug or uncritical satisfaction with oneself.
