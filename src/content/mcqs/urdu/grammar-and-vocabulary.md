  - id: 1
    question: In traditional Urdu grammar words are divided into ism, fail and harf. How is a 'harf' defined?
    options:
      - A word that names a substance, place or quality
      - A word that cannot by itself be resolved into a noun or a verb
      - A word that always carries a case marker such as 'ne' or 'ko'
      - A word borrowed from Persian that keeps its original plural
    answer: 1
    explanation: "A harf (particle) is defined negatively in the classical scheme: it is neither ism (noun) nor fail (verb) and has no meaning unless joined to other words — for example par, se, ko, tak, ke. The first option is the definition of ism, not harf."
    keyConcept: Ism, fail, harf — the three-fold classical division of Urdu words
    examTip: If a word can take a case marker or a tense ending it is an ism or a fail; the residue that can do neither is the harf.
    testedIn:
      - PMS
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
  - id: 2
    question: In the sentence 'Salma ne chitthi likhi', why is the verb 'likhi' feminine singular?
    options:
      - Because the subject Salma is feminine
      - Because verbs in the past tense are always feminine
      - Because adjectives and verbs agree with the postposition used
      - Because with a transitive verb in the past the verb agrees with the object 'chitthi'
    answer: 3
    explanation: In the definite past of a transitive verb the agent takes the marker 'ne' and the verb agrees in gender and number with the object, here the feminine noun chitthi — hence likhi, not likha. Option 1 is the usual rule for other tenses and is the trap.
    keyConcept: Object agreement in the Urdu transitive past (agentive 'ne' construction)
    examTip: "Check three things in order: is the verb transitive, is the tense past, and is the subject marked with 'ne'? Only then does the verb follow the object."
    testedIn:
      - PMS
      - PPSC
  - id: 3
    question: The agentive case marker 'ne' is used in Urdu when the sentence has which kind of verb?
    options:
      - A transitive verb in a past tense
      - An intransitive verb in the present tense
      - A verb in the imperative mood
      - Any verb in the future tense
    answer: 0
    explanation: "'Ne' marks the agent only with transitive verbs in the past (simple and perfect) tenses: 'main ne khat likha'. With intransitive verbs, and in present or future tenses, the subject takes no 'ne' and the verb agrees with the subject."
    keyConcept: Distribution of the Urdu agentive marker and tense-transitivity interaction
    examTip: "'Woh aaya' (intransitive, no ne) versus 'usne bulaya' (transitive past, with ne) is the standard contrast examiners use."
    testedIn:
      - CSS
      - PMS
      - PPSC
  - id: 4
    question: In Urdu several letters share one pronunciation. Which set of four letters is pronounced identically, making the choice between them a classic spelling question?
    options:
      - ت ، ط ، ٹ ، ب
      - س ، ص ، ث ، ش
      - ذ ، ز ، ظ ، ض
      - ہ ، ء ، ے ، ی
    answer: 2
    explanation: All four letters of the third set are pronounced as 'z' in Urdu, although each is a distinct letter in Arabic. The second set fails because ش is 'sh', the first mixes the emphatic ط, the retroflex ٹ and ب 'b', and the fourth set lists the glottal hamza and the ye and he letters.
    keyConcept: Homophonous Urdu letters and spelling discrimination — ذ ز ظ ض for 'z', س ص ث for 's'
    examTip: Learn the 'z' quartet and the 's' trio as separate lists; ٹ is a genuine retroflex and is never a homophone of ت or ط.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - PMS
  - id: 5
    question: A 'murakkab-e-izafi' (ezafe compound) in Urdu is formed when
    options:
      - Two nouns are joined by the coordinating sound 'o' expressing division or pairing
      - One noun is joined to another by the izafat sound 'eh', the pair then behaving as a single noun
      - An adjective is placed directly before the noun it qualifies
      - A verb stem is combined with an auxiliary to show aspect
    answer: 1
    explanation: The izafi compound links two nouns through the Persian genitive vowel 'eh', exactly as in Divan-e-Mir or Bang-e-Dra, and the whole unit then takes case markers as a single noun. Option 1 describes the murakkab-e-taqsimi, option 3 the sifa-mawsuf, and option 4 a verbal compound.
    keyConcept: Izafat construction versus taqsimi, sifa-mawsuf and verbal compounds
    examTip: "Hear the 'eh' sound: if you can insert it between the two words, the compound is izafi; if the words are simply bolted together with 'o', it is taqsimi."
    testedIn:
      - CSS
      - PMS
  - id: 6
    question: In a 'sifa-mawsuf' construction, where does the qualifying word (sifa) normally stand in Urdu?
    options:
      - Before the mawsuf (noun) it qualifies
      - After the mawsuf, as in English
      - Inside the verb phrase of the sentence
      - Anywhere, with no fixed position
    answer: 0
    explanation: "Urdu adjectives are pre-nominal in the ordinary construction: acha larka, purani kitab. Postposition is confined mainly to Persian-style izafi formations, so 'after the noun' is the tempting but wrong answer."
    keyConcept: Sifa-mawsuf (adjective-noun) order and agreement
    examTip: The adjective itself does not change for the gender or number of the noun; only the noun and, in some tenses, the verb change.
    testedIn:
      - PMS
      - PPSC
  - id: 7
    question: Most masculine Urdu nouns ending in the long vowel 'a' (for example larka, chhatra) form the plural by
    options:
      - Adding the Persian ending 'an' to the stem
      - Keeping the stem unchanged and marking plurality on the verb only
      - Changing the final long 'a' to 'e' (larka becomes larke)
      - Adding the feminine ending 'iyan'
    answer: 2
    explanation: "The regular masculine inflection replaces the final long 'a' with 'e': larka becomes larke, chhatra becomes chhatre. Option 1 gives the Persian-style plural such as mardan and option 4 the feminine 'iyan' plural, both of which belong to other noun classes."
    keyConcept: Number in Urdu — inflectional plurals by gender class
    examTip: "Class first, ending second: masculine a-stems take -e, feminine consonant stems take -ein/-en (kitab/kitaben), and Arabic break plurals must be memorised."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
  - id: 8
    question: The Urdu idiom 'bher ke aage been bajana' means
    options:
      - To announce a festival with music in the countryside
      - To waste speech on petty quarrels in public
      - To sing devotional songs before an unlearned audience
      - To spend effort pleading with someone wholly incapable of understanding or appreciating it
    answer: 3
    explanation: "The idiom denounces futile appeal: playing a reed-pipe before a buffalo that cannot value the music. Options 1 and 3 keep the literal musical scene and are the trap; the point is the recipient's incapacity, not the music."
    keyConcept: Mutloka/muhawra — figurative sense overrides literal components
    examTip: In idiom MCQs discard every option that merely restates the literal picture; the answer almost always names the human situation.
    testedIn:
      - PMS
      - PPSC
      - FPSC

    question: What is the meaning of the Urdu idiom 'naak katna'?
    options:
      - To be put to shame and lose one's honour
      - To cut an argument short rudely
      - To suffer a serious physical injury
      - To refuse a demand flatly
    answer: 0
    explanation: "'Naak katna' is used for utter disgrace or loss of prestige, not for any bodily harm. The literal-injury option is the trap; Urdu idiom questions expect the transferred meaning of badnami."
    keyConcept: Body-part idioms (naak, kaan, dil, haath) used figuratively
    examTip: Body-part idioms in Urdu almost always signify honour, emotion or power, never the physical act named.

  - id: 10
    question: The idiom 'namak haram karna' is used for
    options:
      - Cooking a dish without adding salt
      - Cheating in a commercial measurement of weight
      - Acting ungratefully and betraying kindness received
      - Making an empty or false promise about the future
    answer: 2
    explanation: Salt (namak) stands for the kindness and sustenance received from a patron, so 'namak haram' means ingratitude and betrayal; its opposite is 'namak halali' (loyalty). The food and trade options keep the literal sense of salt and are the distractors.
    keyConcept: Idioms of loyalty and ingratitude built on a cultural symbol
    examTip: Pair it with 'namak halali' as its antonym; commissions like testing the two in one paper.
    testedIn:
      - PMS
      - PPSC
  - id: 11
    question: In Urdu administrative vocabulary, the word 'mulazim' means
    options:
      - A resignation accepted by a superior
      - An employee or servant held in engagement
      - A promotion granted on seniority
      - An official seal affixed to a dispatch
    answer: 1
    explanation: Mulazim (ملازم) denotes a person engaged in service, i.e. an employee or servant, and 'sarkari mulazim' is a government servant. The other options are service-related words from the same field but carry different senses.
    keyConcept: Perso-Arabic administrative vocabulary used in Urdu official prose
    examTip: "Learn the opposed pairs in this vocabulary: taayyun and azl (appointment and dismissal), afsar and mulazim (officer and subordinate employee)."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
  - id: 12
    question: Which statement correctly describes how Urdu relates a noun to the words around it?
    options:
      - It places prepositions before the noun, as English does
      - It marks the relation by conjugating the main verb
      - It uses a definite article placed before the noun
      - It follows an object-verb order and places postpositions after the noun
    answer: 3
    explanation: Urdu is a subject-object-verb language with postpositions (ghar mein, larkay ko, darakht se); the relation is signalled after the noun. Urdu has no definite article, so option 3 is wrong, and options 1 and 2 describe English or inflected languages.
    keyConcept: Urdu typology — SOV word order and postpositional case relations
    examTip: Every translation item on 'ka/ke/kii', 'ko', 'se', 'mein', 'tak' is really testing whether you know these are postpositions governed by the harf class.
    testedIn:
      - CSS
      - PMS
      - PPSC
