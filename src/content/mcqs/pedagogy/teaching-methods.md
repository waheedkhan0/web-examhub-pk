  - id: 1
    question: In Benjamin Bloom's original 1956 taxonomy of the cognitive domain, which level stands at the top?
    options:
      - Application
      - Synthesis
      - Evaluation
      - Comprehension
    answer: 2
    explanation: The original order is Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation, so Evaluation is the highest level. Synthesis is the tempting runner-up; it becomes the top only when 'Creating' is renamed in the revised taxonomy.
    keyConcept: Bloom's cognitive domain hierarchy and its revised form
    examTip: "Note whether the paper says 'original' or 'revised': in the 2001 revision Creating (not Evaluation) is the highest level."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 2
    question: In the revised Bloom's taxonomy, which level follows immediately after 'Applying'?
    options:
      - Remembering
      - Analysing
      - Understanding
      - Creating
    answer: 1
    explanation: The revised sequence is Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create, so Analysing comes directly after Applying. Understanding precedes Applying, which makes option 3 a backward-ordering trap.
    keyConcept: Revised (Anderson and Krathwohl) cognitive process hierarchy
    examTip: Write the six verbs as a ladder twice a day; most PET questions only test the order and the neighbour of a given level.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 3
    question: Which of the following is NOT one of the five formal steps of the Herbartian lesson plan?
    options:
      - Brainstorming
      - Preparation
      - Presentation
      - Association (comparison)
    answer: 0
    explanation: Herbart's steps run from preparation through presentation, association, generalisation to application; brainstorming is Osborn's later group-idea technique and belongs to no Herbartian list. The other three are genuine steps, which is why option 1 must be read as the exception.
    keyConcept: Herbart's five formal steps of instruction
    examTip: Any option naming a modern participatory technique (brainstorming, buzz groups) is the odd one out in a Herbart question.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
  - id: 4
    question: Which sequence correctly represents the 5E instructional model?
    options:
      - Engage, Explain, Explore, Elaborate, Evaluate
      - Explore, Engage, Explain, Evaluate, Elaborate
      - Engage, Explore, Elaborate, Explain, Evaluate
      - Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate
    answer: 3
    explanation: "The constructivist 5E cycle is Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate (or Extend), Evaluate: learners meet the idea before it is formally explained. Option 1 is the trap because it puts explanation before exploration, reversing the model's logic."
    keyConcept: 5E constructivist lesson structure (Bybee)
    examTip: Remember that in 5E the teacher's explanation comes third, after learners have explored — unlike the Herbartian order.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
      - FPSC
  - id: 5
    question: Micro-teaching is best described as
    options:
      - A method of teaching small printed illustrations to young children
      - A teacher-training technique that isolates one skill, uses a scaled-down class and short lesson, then feedback and re-teaching
      - A revision test conducted at the end of each short unit of work
      - Teaching a mixed-age class in a single classroom
    answer: 1
    explanation: Micro-teaching is a supervised-practice procedure (developed in the 1960s) in which a trainee teaches a small group for a few minutes, receives feedback and re-plans, concentrating on one skill such as set induction or questioning. Option 4 describes multigrade teaching, a different concept entirely.
    keyConcept: Micro-teaching cycle and isolated teaching skills
    examTip: "Key words examiners look for: skill-focused, scaled-down, feedback, re-teach."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 6
    question: What is the main limitation of the demonstration method of teaching?
    options:
      - The learner observes a performed procedure but does not necessarily handle or practise the skill himself
      - It cannot be used for topics that require laboratory apparatus
      - It demands far more prior preparation than a lecture
      - It is unsuitable for large classes because it needs individual desks
    answer: 0
    explanation: In a demonstration the teacher or a pupil performs while others watch, so learning stays at observation unless followed by hands-on practice. It is actually the method of choice where apparatus is scarce, so options 2 and 4 are wrong.
    keyConcept: Demonstration versus experiment — passive observation risk
    examTip: Demonstration shows, experiment does; a question about 'learning by doing' points to the experimental or project method instead.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 7
    question: The project method received its classic statement in the 1918 essay 'The Project Method' written by
    options:
      - Johann Friedrich Herbart
      - Friedrich Froebel
      - William Heard Kilpatrick
      - Maria Montessori
    answer: 2
    explanation: Kilpatrick, working within Dewey's pragmatic tradition at Teachers College Columbia, formulated the project method in his 1918 essay. Dewey supplies the philosophy but did not write that essay, so the education-theorist distractors all name different programmes.
    keyConcept: Project method and learning-through-purposeful-activity tradition
    examTip: "Attribution ladder: Dewey (philosophy) then Kilpatrick (classic statement of the project method)."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 8
    question: Assessment carried out continuously during instruction in order to spot learning gaps and feed the learner back is called
    options:
      - Summative assessment
      - Aptitude assessment
      - Placement assessment
      - Formative assessment
    answer: 3
    explanation: Formative assessment is embedded in teaching, low-stakes and diagnostic in purpose, aimed at improving learning while it is still happening. Summative assessment instead certifies achievement at the end of a course, which is the opposite function.
    keyConcept: Formative versus summative evaluation
    examTip: The clue words are 'during instruction' and 'feedback' for formative, 'at the end' and 'grading' for summative.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 9
    question: A mark interpreted by comparing a candidate with other candidates in the same group shows what kind of referencing?
    options:
      - Norm-referenced interpretation
      - Criterion-referenced interpretation
      - Ipsative interpretation
      - Absolute-standard interpretation
    answer: 0
    explanation: Norm-referenced interpretation places the learner on a distribution built from the group, as in grading on a curve or percentile ranks. Criterion-referenced interpretation (option 2) judges the score against a fixed mastery standard independent of other candidates.
    keyConcept: Norm-referenced versus criterion-referenced test scores
    examTip: Words like 'percentile', 'relative position' and 'curve' signal norm-referenced; 'mastery', 'cut-off mark' signal criterion-referenced.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 10
    question: A test that produces almost the same score on every re-administration yet measures something other than the intended objective is best described as
    options:
      - Valid but not reliable
      - Neither valid nor reliable
      - Reliable but not valid
      - Both reliable and valid
    answer: 2
    explanation: Consistency of results is reliability; measuring what the test claims to measure is validity. A test can repeat its error faithfully, so it is reliable without being valid; the reverse combination in option 1 is not what the stem describes.
    keyConcept: Reliability and validity as separate test qualities
    examTip: Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity — a favourite true/false item in PET and NTS papers.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
      - FPSC
  - id: 11
    question: When selecting a teaching-learning material (TLM) for a lesson, the first criterion a teacher should apply is
    options:
      - Whether it is cheap and easy to procure
      - Whether it directly serves the stated learning objectives of that lesson
      - Whether it is visually attractive and new to the class
      - Whether it is suitable for display on a projector
    answer: 1
    explanation: Aids are selected to bridge a specific learning gap towards a specific objective; cost, novelty and format are secondary constraints judged only after that match. Option 1 is the tempting practical answer but is a limitation, not a criterion of appropriateness.
    keyConcept: Objective-driven selection and use of teaching-learning material
    examTip: In any 'most important' pedagogy question, the answer tied to objectives outranks answers tied to cost, interest or technology.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 12
    question: In a behavioural objective written in the ABCD format, what does the letter 'D' specify?
    options:
      - The domain of learning to which the objective belongs
      - The duration allotted for teaching the content
      - The differentiation required for slow learners
      - The degree or standard of performance that will be accepted as evidence of learning
    answer: 3
    explanation: "ABCD stands for Audience, Behaviour, Condition and Degree: the degree states how well the learner must perform, such as 'eight out of ten' or 'without error'. Domain (option 1) belongs to Bloom's classification, not to the objective format."
    keyConcept: ABCD components of a measurable behavioural objective
    examTip: If a sample objective lacks a measurable criterion, the missing element is the Degree; if it lacks 'given a map', the missing element is the Condition.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
