    question: Which sequence correctly lists Piaget's stages of cognitive development with their approximate age ranges?
    options:
      - Sensorimotor (0-2), Concrete operational (2-7), Preoperational (7-11), Formal operational (12+)
      - Sensorimotor (0-2), Preoperational (2-7), Concrete operational (7-11), Formal operational (12+)
      - Preoperational (0-2), Sensorimotor (2-7), Formal operational (7-11), Concrete operational (12+)
      - Sensorimotor (0-7), Preoperational (7-11), Concrete operational (11-15), Formal operational (15+)
    answer: 1
    explanation: Piaget's order is sensorimotor (birth to about two), preoperational (two to seven), concrete operational (about seven to eleven) and formal operational (from about twelve). Option 1 swaps the middle two stages, the most common error, and option 4 shifts the age boundaries.
    keyConcept: Piaget's four stages in order with age bands
    examTip: "Use the mnemonic S-P-C-F and anchor it to school: preoperational = nursery and KG, concrete = primary, formal = secondary."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 2
    question: A child of about eight understands that pouring water from a short wide glass into a tall narrow one does not change the amount. This ability is called
    options:
      - Animism
      - Egocentrism
      - Centration
      - Conservation
    answer: 3
    explanation: Conservation is the understanding that quantity stays constant despite a change in appearance, and it is the hallmark of the concrete operational stage. Animism attributes life to objects, egocentrism is the inability to take another's viewpoint, and centration is fixing on one feature — all preoperational characteristics.
    keyConcept: Conservation as the criterion for concrete operational thought
    examTip: If the stem mentions shape change and quantity remaining the same, answer conservation; if it mentions only one dimension, answer centration.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
      - FPSC
  - id: 3
    question: The gap between what a learner can do alone and what the learner can achieve with guidance from a more capable person is called
    options:
      - The Zone of Proximal Development
      - Scaffolding
      - Assimilation
      - The critical period
    answer: 0
    explanation: Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development names that difference between actual and potential development. Scaffolding (option 2) is the support given inside the zone, not the zone itself — the pairing most often tested.
    keyConcept: Vygotsky's ZPD and the social origin of higher mental functions
    examTip: ZPD is a distance, scaffolding is a technique; read the stem for 'gap' versus 'support'.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 4
    question: Temporary support given by a teacher or skilled peer and progressively withdrawn as the learner gains independence is known as
    options:
      - Shaping
      - Modelling
      - Scaffolding
      - Generalisation
    answer: 2
    explanation: Scaffolding is the contingent, fading assistance that lets a child work within the ZPD until the skill is internalised. Shaping (option 1) reinforces successive approximations behaviourally and never refers to withdrawal of cognitive support.
    keyConcept: Scaffolding as graduated, fading support
    examTip: Look for the word 'withdrawn' or 'faded' in the stem — that is scaffolding, not mere help.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 5
    question: According to Erikson, the central psychosocial crisis of adolescence is
    options:
      - Identity versus role confusion
      - Industry versus inferiority
      - Initiative versus guilt
      - Integrity versus despair
    answer: 0
    explanation: Erikson placed the formation of a coherent ego identity, against diffuse or confused roles, at the adolescent stage. Industry versus inferiority belongs to the school years and integrity versus despair to old age, so both are familiar-name traps.
    keyConcept: Erikson's psychosocial stages mapped to life periods
    examTip: "Match crisis to school level: trust (infancy), autonomy (early childhood), initiative (play age), industry (primary), identity (secondary)."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 6
    question: A pupil who calls an act wrong only because the actor will be punished is reasoning at which of Kohlberg's levels?
    options:
      - Postconventional
      - Conventional
      - Autonomous morality
      - Preconventional
    answer: 3
    explanation: Punishment-avoidance and self-interest reasoning define the preconventional level, its first stage. Conventional reasoning appeals to approval and law and order, and postconventional reasoning to social contract and universal principles.
    keyConcept: Kohlberg's three levels of moral reasoning
    examTip: "Anchor words: punishment or reward = preconventional; 'what people think' or 'rules' = conventional; rights and principles = postconventional."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
      - FPSC
  - id: 7
    question: In Pavlov's experiment, after the bell has been repeatedly paired with food, the salivation elicited by the bell alone is the
    options:
      - Unconditioned response
      - Conditioned response
      - Unconditioned stimulus
      - Neutral stimulus
    answer: 1
    explanation: A response that originally appeared only to the unconditioned stimulus (food) and now appears to the previously neutral bell is the conditioned response. The bell itself, once paired, becomes the conditioned stimulus — the substitution examiners use as the trap.
    keyConcept: Classical conditioning — UCS, UCR, CS, CR and extinction
    examTip: "Stimulus versus response: if the stem names a behaviour (salivation, fear), answer a response; if it names a signal, answer a stimulus."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 8
    question: Switching off an annoying noise whenever a student works quietly, so that quiet working increases, is an example of
    options:
      - Positive reinforcement
      - Punishment
      - Negative reinforcement
      - Extinction
    answer: 2
    explanation: Negative reinforcement strengthens a behaviour by removing an aversive stimulus; the behaviour increases. Punishment is the trap because the word 'negative' suggests something unpleasant is added, but anything that increases a behaviour is reinforcement.
    keyConcept: Skinner's operant consequences — reinforcement versus punishment
    examTip: "Ask two questions: does behaviour increase or decrease, and is something given or taken away? Only 'increase plus removal' is negative reinforcement."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
      - FPSC
  - id: 9
    question: Which of Thorndike's laws of learning states that a connection is strengthened when the response is followed by a satisfying state of affairs?
    options:
      - The law of effect
      - The law of readiness
      - The law of exercise
      - The law of multiple response
    answer: 0
    explanation: The law of effect makes reward the engine of stamping-in, and the law of exercise makes repetition the engine of strength. Readiness concerns the learner's preparedness, so option 2 is the frequent but wrong answer.
    keyConcept: Thorndike's connectionism and the three primary laws
    examTip: Effect = consequence, exercise = practice, readiness = preparedness; map each to a classroom example before the test.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 10
    question: A child acquires a new behaviour by watching a model perform it and seeing the model rewarded. Bandura explains this as
    options:
      - Insight learning
      - Latent learning
      - Vicarious reinforcement through observational learning
      - Stimulus generalisation
    answer: 2
    explanation: Bandura's social learning theory holds that learners acquire behaviour by observing models, and that seeing a model reinforced (vicarious reinforcement) raises the likelihood of imitation. Insight is Kohler's sudden reorganisation and latent learning is Tolman's unexpressed learning, neither of which requires a model.
    keyConcept: Bandura's observational learning — attention, retention, reproduction, motivation
    examTip: The presence of a model plus a consequence observed by the learner always points to Bandura rather than to Skinner's direct reinforcement.
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 11
    question: Which psychologist proposed that the mind contains relatively autonomous intelligences — linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal and naturalistic?
    options:
      - Charles Spearman
      - Howard Gardner
      - Alfred Binet
      - Louis Thurstone
    answer: 1
    explanation: Gardner's framework of multiple intelligences, set out in the 1980s, treats these as distinct intellectual competences. Spearman argued for a single general factor g (option 1), Binet built the first mental-age test, and Thurstone proposed primary mental factors, which is the closest distractor.
    keyConcept: Gardner's multiple intelligences versus unitary g theories
    examTip: "Contrast pairs are tested: Gardner (many intelligences) against Spearman (one g) and Thurstone (primary factors)."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
  - id: 12
    question: Which pairing of a specific learning difficulty with the domain it affects is correct?
    options:
      - Dyslexia — difficulty with number concepts and arithmetic
      - Dyscalculia — difficulty with accurate and fluent handwriting
      - Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder — a difficulty confined to reading fluency
      - Dysgraphia — difficulty with handwriting and written expression
    answer: 3
    explanation: Dysgraphia is the specific difficulty with written expression and handwriting, while dyslexia affects reading and word decoding and dyscalculia affects arithmetic. ADHD is a pattern of inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity across settings, not a reading deficit.
    keyConcept: Specific learning disabilities — dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, ADHD
    examTip: "Read the Greek root: lex = words, graph = writing, calculus = number; commissions simply permute these three across the options."
    testedIn:
      - PPSC
      - FPSC
      - NTS
