    question: The amount of oxygen consumed by the aerobic bacterias which cause the aerobic biological decomposition of sewage, is known
    options:
      - Bio-Chemical Oxygen Demand (B.O.D.)
      - Dissolved Oxygen (D.O.)
      - Chemical Oxygen Demand (C.O.D.)
      - None of these
    answer: 1
    explanation: B.O.D. (Bio-Chemical Oxygen Demand) refers to the amount of oxygen required by aerobic bacteria to decompose organic matter present in sewage. During this process, microorganisms consume oxygen while breaking down organic pollutants biologically. Hence, it directly measures the oxygen consumed by aerobic biological activity in wastewater. D.O. (Dissolved Oxygen) represents the oxygen available in water, not the oxygen consumed. C.O.D. (Chemical Oxygen Demand) measures oxygen required for chemical oxidation of organic matter, not specifically biological decomposition. Therefore, the correct term describing oxygen used by aerobic bacteria is B.O.D.

    question: For design of sewers, percentage of sewage discharge is assumed
    options:
      - 65 to 70%
      - 70 to 75%
      - 75 to 80%
      - 85%
    answer: 2
    explanation: "Let's understand this question step by step. Imagine water coming into your home from the city supply. You use this water for many things: bathing, cooking, flushing toilets, washing clothes, and so on. The used water that goes down the drain and carries waste is called sewage . The underground pipes that collect and carry this sewage are called sewers . Now, when civil engineers design these sewers, they need to know how much sewage they should expect to flow through the pipes so they can make them the correct size. If the pipes are too small, they will overflow. If they are too big, they will be expensive and not work efficiently. Here's the key point: Not all the water supplied to a city or town becomes sewage. Why? Because some water is: - Drunk or used in food preparation and is consumed. - Used for watering gardens and lawns, where it evaporates or soaks into the ground. - Lost due to leakages in the water supply pipes before it even reaches homes. - Used in some industrial processes where water is consumed and not discharged as wastewater. So, engineers make an assumption about what percentage of the total water supplied will actually convert into sewage discharge . This percentage helps them estimate the flow rate for designing the sewers. Based on standard engineering practices and observations, it is generally assumed that about 70% to 80% of the total water supplied to an area will eventually become sewage and needs to be carried by the sewers. Sometimes, this figure can be slightly adjusted based on the specific type of area (e.g., purely residential, commercial, or industrial), but 70-80% is a widely accepted range. Looking at the given options: Option A: 65 to 70% Option B: 70 to 75% Option C: 75 to 80% Option D: 85% The most appropriate and commonly assumed range for the design of sewers is 75 to 80% . This value ensures that the sewers are adequately sized to handle the expected sewage flow, considering the factors mentioned above. Therefore, the correct answer is Option C: 75 to 80% ."

    question: The width of a rectangular sewer is twice its depth while discharging 1.5 m/sec. The width of the sewer is
    options:
      - 0.68 m
      - 0.88 m
      - 1.36 m
      - 1.76 m
    answer: 2
    explanation: "First, let's understand the problem: We have a rectangular sewer (like a rectangular channel). We know the width is twice the depth . We also know the discharge (flow rate) is 1.5 m³/sec. We need to find the width . Here's how we can approach it: 1. Area and Velocity: Remember the relationship: Discharge (Q) = Area (A) * Velocity (V). We know Q (1.5 m³/sec) and V (1.5 m/sec). So, we can find A. 2. Calculate Area: Area (A) = Discharge (Q) / Velocity (V) = 1.5 m³/sec / 1.5 m/sec = 1 m² 3. Relate Width and Depth: Let's say the depth = d . The problem tells us that the width = 2d . 4. Area in terms of 'd': The area of a rectangle is width * depth. So, Area (A) = (2d) * d = 2d² 5. Solve for 'd': We know A = 1 m², and A = 2d². Therefore, 2d² = 1 d² = 1/2 = 0.5 d = √0.5 ≈ 0.707 m 6. Find the Width: Remember, width = 2d. So, Width = 2 * 0.707 m ≈ 1.414 m 7. Choose the closest answer: The closest option to 1.414 m is Option C: 1.36 m . This is because in real-world scenarios, there might be some approximations or rounding involved. So, C is the best choice. Therefore, the correct answer is Option C: 1.36 m"

    question: Kjeldahl nitrogen is a mixture of
    options:
      - Ammonia and nitrogen
      - Nitrogen and organic nitrogen
      - Organic nitrogen and ammonia
      - All the above
    answer: 3
    explanation: "Kjeldahl Nitrogen (TKN) is the total amount of nitrogen present in the form of organic nitrogen and ammonia in wastewater. Think of wastewater as containing nitrogen in different forms. Some nitrogen is still present inside waste materials such as food particles, proteins, human waste, and plant or animal remains . This is called organic nitrogen . As these waste materials start decomposing, a part of the organic nitrogen is converted into ammonia (NH₃/NH₄⁺) . Therefore, wastewater mainly contains nitrogen in these two forms— organic nitrogen and ammonia . The Kjeldahl method is a laboratory test that measures the combined amount of organic nitrogen and ammonia nitrogen . This combined value is known as Total Kjeldahl Nitrogen (TKN) . Easy Trick to Remember: TKN = Organic Nitrogen + Ammonia Nitrogen The test does not measure nitrate (NO₃⁻) or nitrite (NO₂⁻) . These are different forms of nitrogen and are analyzed using separate laboratory tests. Hence, Kjeldahl Nitrogen is a mixture of Organic Nitrogen and Ammonia , making Option C the correct answer."

    question: Stone ware pipes are
    options:
      - Highly resistant to sulphide corrosion
      - Highly impervious
      - Hydraulically efficient because of their smooth interior surface
      - Especially suited to pressure pipes
    answer: 0
    explanation: "The correct answer is Option A: Highly resistant to sulphide corrosion. In Wastewater Engineering , pipes carry sewage, which can be quite harsh. One of the biggest problems is sulphide corrosion . Here's how it happens: 1. Wastewater contains materials that can be broken down by tiny bacteria. 2. These bacteria produce a gas called hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) . 3. When this gas mixes with moisture inside the pipe, especially near the top, it can form sulphuric acid . 4. Sulphuric acid is highly corrosive and can damage materials such as concrete over time. Stoneware pipes are made from a special type of clay that is baked at very high temperatures. This process makes them dense, hard, and chemically inert . Chemically inert means they do not react easily with chemicals, including strong acids such as sulphuric acid. Therefore, stoneware pipes are highly resistant to sulphide corrosion . This is a major advantage because they can provide long service life in the corrosive environment of sewerage systems. Option B: Highly impervious This statement is also a good property of stoneware pipes . Stoneware is dense and has low permeability, which helps prevent water from passing through the pipe walls. However, resistance to sulphide corrosion is the more relevant distinguishing property in this question. Option C: Hydraulically efficient because of their smooth interior surface Stoneware pipes generally have a smooth interior surface , which helps wastewater flow with relatively low friction. However, hydraulic efficiency is not their most distinctive advantage, as several other pipe materials also provide smooth internal surfaces. Option D: Especially suited to pressure pipes This statement is incorrect . Stoneware pipes are relatively brittle and can crack or break when subjected to high internal pressure or impact. They are mainly used in gravity-flow sewerage systems rather than pressure pipelines. Hence, the correct answer is Option A: Highly resistant to sulphide corrosion."

This arrangement helps maintain proper hydraulic conditions and reduces the possibility of backwater entering the smaller sewer. Option A: Top of smaller sewer is kept lower Incorrect. The standard rule is expressed in terms of the smaller sewer's soffit being at the same level or higher than that of the larger sewer, rather than stating that the larger sewer's top is always kept lower. Option C: Tops of both the sewers are at the same level Incorrect. The tops may be at the same level in some arrangements, but this is not the general rule for every sewer junction. Option D: None of these Correct, because none of the first three options states the standard rule accurately and generally. Hence, Option D — None of these is the correct answer."
